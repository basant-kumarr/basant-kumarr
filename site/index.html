Problem

02

Flagship AI product, fitness and nutrition

BeBeyond In development

An AI fitness and nutrition platform I designed and built end to end, from the mobile app through to a deployed API. The interesting problem was not the modelling. It was deciding what the AI is allowed to do.

17database tables
~15klines of backend Python
106test files across the stack
Private repository

The decision that shaped the product

The coach runs on a deterministic engine that owns every number: readiness, accumulated load, nutrition targets, and what to train next. The language model is only allowed to write the sentence that explains that decision. It cannot change a target, invent a readiness score, or fill in a confidence value.

I built it that way because every signal in the app carries a confidence level, including "unavailable", and people make real training decisions from those numbers. A model asked to fill in a schema will produce a plausible value every time, and a plausible readiness score is worse than a blank one. If the model call fails, the coach falls back to the deterministic answer, so it degrades instead of breaking.

That is the same argument I would make for putting a model into any regulated process.

Problem

Fitness advice is scattered and mostly generic. Your training plan lives in one app, your food tracking in another, and neither knows what you did yesterday or how recovered you are today. You end up with a lot of data and very little guidance.

Product

BeBeyond puts training, nutrition and coaching in one place. The part that matters is that it makes a recommendation and can tell you exactly why it made it.

What is built and working

  • Exercise ontology. A structured model of exercises, the muscles each one loads and how they relate. Because the relationships are modelled rather than hardcoded, the system can reason about substitutions and whether a training week is balanced, instead of just listing movements.
  • Muscle load and readiness. Tracks accumulated load per muscle group and turns it into a readiness signal. Every signal carries a confidence tier, and an unavailable signal stays visibly unavailable rather than being filled in with an estimate.
  • Recommendation engine. Takes readiness and recent load and works out what to train next and what to leave alone. Recommendations carry the evidence they came from, which is what lets the coach explain itself.
  • Nutrition engine. Works against the same profile and state the training side uses, so the two cannot give contradictory advice.
  • AI Coach. Answers training and nutrition questions grounded in current state, with a pluggable provider layer and a deterministic fallback.
  • 3D anatomical visualisation. Renders the movement and the muscles it targets, so the ontology is something you can see rather than a table in a database.
  • Platform. 17 tables covering programmes, sessions, performances, nutrition, habits, coach memory and health summaries, with versioned migrations, authentication with session revocation, rate limiting, and a documented pre-launch security pass.

Not finished yet, and labelled that way

  • Camera based form analysis. The analysis pipeline is built and tested: rep detection, joint angles, range of motion, tempo and form rules. It currently runs against a simulated pose source. On device inference is not wired up, because the camera frame processor stack conflicts with a package the app already depends on.
  • Wearables. Apple Health and Health Connect adapters are scaffolded. No live wearable data flows yet, and the app marks those signals unavailable rather than estimating them.

I list these separately on purpose. The codebase labels simulated data as simulated, and this page should not be less careful than the code is.

This one is entirely mine. I designed the data model, wrote the backend, built the mobile app and made the calls about what the system is allowed to claim.

Most of the work here has not been the modelling. It has been deciding what the product should recommend, what it is allowed to claim, and what the data model needs to look like to support that. 04

Machine learning, explainable AI

Financial Fraud Detection

Logistic regression against a tuned XGBoost model on a public transaction dataset, using SMOTE for the class imbalance and SHAP to explain what was driving each flag.

437more caught than the baseline, in testing
99.9%AUC-ROC
97.2%precision
Repository

How to read those numbers

Those are high figures and they should be read with the dataset in mind. This is a public Kaggle dataset, not proprietary card network data, and it is cleaner and more separable than real transaction data, so the ceiling is far higher than anything you would see in production. Fraud is also very rare in the data, which is why I used SMOTE to rebalance the training set, and why precision matters more here than raw accuracy.

The interesting result was not the score. It was the 437 cases in testing that the logistic regression baseline missed and the tuned model caught, and what SHAP showed about why they were missed.

Problem

Baseline fraud models catch the obvious cases and miss the rest. The question was how many more a tuned model catches, and whether the catches can be explained well enough for anyone in a regulated setting to act on them.

What I did

Compared logistic regression against a tuned XGBoost model, rebalanced the training data with SMOTE because genuine fraud is a tiny fraction of the rows, then used SHAP to attribute each flag to the features driving it.

My role

A personal project, start to finish. I chose the dataset, the baseline, the resampling approach and the evaluation.

What it demonstrates

Regulated industries cannot use a model they cannot explain. Pairing the performance work with SHAP was the point of the project, not an afterthought.

What I would do differently

That a headline score can be the least interesting number in the work. Once I looked at which cases the baseline missed, the project got a lot more useful and a lot more honest.

My role

Engineering capstone, group project

Swarm Drone Coordination

My final year engineering project, built by a team. Multiple drones coordinating on a shared task, with position sharing over radio and simple rules for who does what and how they stay out of each other's way.

Teamfinal year project
2020selected for the India Innovation Challenge

Problem

One drone doing a job is a control problem. Several drones doing it together is a coordination problem, and that is a different and much harder thing.

What the team built

Arduino based units sharing GPS position over RF, with task allocation deciding which unit takes which part of the job and basic collision avoidance keeping them apart.

My role

I worked on this as part of the project team. I am deliberately not claiming individual credit for specific subsystems here, because it was genuinely a group build and I would rather describe it accurately than impressively.

What it demonstrates

This is where the systems thinking started. Coordinating independent units under incomplete information is the same shape of problem as coordinating a process across departments, which is what I do now with far less hardware involved.
